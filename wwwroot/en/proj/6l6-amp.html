Introduction

It all started with two coincidences.

  1. During my training I had access to tubes
  2. A friend of mine was familiar with them

And because I thought it couldn't be that difficult, I digged a bit into the tube theme. Unlike transistors, these glass cylinders are designed for high voltages, a topic that was new to me at the time.

So I taught myself with the help of my friend Christian and searched on Jogis Röhrenbude for circuits that involved the tubes I had access to.

Relatively early on, two types of tubes stood out, for which there was already a Schematic: the 6L6 and the EF86.

Schematic

The schematic itself was taken more or less 1:1 from Jogi. One change I made:
At the output of the transformers I connected a magic eye EM84 as a rough VU meter.

Otherwise, the circuit is relatively simple. The EF86 pentode functions as a preamplifier tube and drives a 6L6 power tube in single-ended mode. Do this twice and you have a stereo amplifier.

Typical for tubes is, of course, the comparatively high supply voltage of about 350 volts. So my advice here: be careful and only work with it if you know what you are doing!

Transformers

Now the big question: where to source the transformers from? And here I was very lucky! In the institute where I was during my training, there were not only tubes, but also a transformer winding machine and rolls of suitable copper wire. In addition, Jogi's site even provides the dimensions for the transformers! So I ordered EI laminations and winding bodies, the winding machine was awakened from its deep sleep and serviced, and I got to winding them.

Since my supervisor was also interested in the project, I was allowed to use all the materials if I also built an amplifier for him. So two sets of transformers were wound at the same time.

Housing

The next step was to build a suitable housing. However, at that time I was neither experienced nor skilled in woodworking. So I chose the simple option: 20mm aluminum extrusion profile with steel plates as top, bottom, and side panels. I ordered the plates from a supplier, and the profiles were also ordered to size. The result was that the housing was quickly assembled, but visually very plain and also not lightweight.

It is certainly not the most beautiful amplifier. But, in the words of an acquaintance: "At least. If the amplifier ever drops, it is not the amplifier that is damaged, but the floor."

Wiring

The first decision was an error. During my training, I was obsessed with PCBs and designed the amplifier on a PCB. In retrospect, not a very good idea, not only because of the high voltages. I also had little knowledge of clearance distances, etc., as it was my first project outside of extra-low voltage. The PCB itself surprisingly lasted about a year before it eventually burned and arced over.

After that incident, I decided to rebuild the amplifier with terminal strips instead of a new PCB. So terminal strips were ordered, the components were soldered onto them, and all other connections were made with wires. The interior now looks more like a real tube amplifier and no longer like an amateur DIY project, but I am sure there is still much room for improvement.

Summary

Now the question: Does the amp actually work? Answer: Of course! It had many problems at the beginning, but these have now mostly been resolved. A Bluetooth module was also added later, so I can also play music via my phone.

The amplifier itself is somewhat bass-heavy, but that doesn't bother me all that much. I use it as an amplifier for my TV with JBL LX 800 speakers. Even though the amplifier itself only has 10W, it makes good pressure with the speakers and has a good sound profile.
